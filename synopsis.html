Synopsis

The Magic Pick

The premise

The Magic Pick is a guitar pick, made from an unknown, possible extra-terrestrial, substance that has passed from hand to hand for generations, absorbing something from every player who ever held it and pouring all of them out through whoever holds it next. Hold it and out comes an immersive experience, filling the room with responsive visuals and a highly produced sound — the accumulated talent of everyone who came before. In return it captures the musical DNA of the player.

It is, in other words, AI music-making software — imagined fifty years early. The wow factor is real: the show never pretends the pick doesn't sound incredible. The bill is real too. And the audience in the club splits exactly the way listeners are splitting right now, in the middle of the AI-music controversy: some go for the glitz — the huge processed sound, the light show, the instant spectacle — and some want the natural, unprocessed thing played by a human being who bled for it. The show stages both and honors both. Its hero simply decides, the hard way, which musician he wants to be.

This is a guitar musical — the guitar is the protagonist's voice, the temptation, and the redemption — told as a stoner comedy: the 70s drugs, drinking, betrayal and the price of the shortcut. Though the show's 23 songs were written in the 1970s and are preserved exactly as they were written, the story has arrived, unaltered, as a parable for the age of artificial intelligence: a man seduced by the artificiality of the pick who discovers there is no substitute for authenticity — and, after the wow factor fades, goes back to making music the old-fashioned way.

Act One

Backstage in a 1976 Toronto club, ROBBIE — mid-30s, gifted, bitter, discouraged and drunk — lays out his sentence: fifteen years of "Mustang Sally," a human jukebox in the graveyard of ambition, exiled from Nova Scotia and the wife he should have fought for (How Fair She Grows). His band hauls him off a road case with a warning that the manager is counting reasons to stiff them (Sleeping in the Studio), and he fronts the cover band he despises through a barn-burner the crowd loves and he doesn't (Juke Box Land).

After the set, MELISSA shoulders past security — formidable, slightly loaded, armor over something shaken. She has brought somebody who can help him. The old spark arcs between rage and longing (You Unlocked Me). Then CLIVE is simply there — barefoot, beads, a smile from 1967. He introduces himself in song (Glad to Meetcha) and opens his hand on the pick, telling the truth about it: every hand that held it left a little something in it, but it also takes in what you’re playing. Robbie is willing to do anything to get noticed - he'll sign in blood — and recognizing his desperation, the whole COMPANY surrounds him singing (Poor Boy).

The artificial delivers everything it promised — the room explodes, the crowd goes for the glitz, and for the first time in fifteen years Robbie sounds like a star. So does his ambition (I Don't Care Who I Step On, On My Way Down). He bristles at the strange old man shadowing him; Clive answers with an offer of kinship — not a big brother, a grand-brother (I'm Your Grand-Brother). Warming despite himself, Robbie pours out the real wound: a career spent knocking on a door dead-bolted from the inside (Why Won't They Play My Song). Melissa confesses the thing under her anger — sometimes she hates him, but she loves his music (You'll Always Sound Right to Me). When Robbie snarls that Clive knows nothing, the old man proves otherwise, singing Robbie's whole buried life back to him — the wife, the blue-eyed daughter crying on the telephone, the cool blue water (Nova Scotia Goodbye).

Melissa announces she's going home on the morning train. Robbie goes to his knees (Please Baby Don't Make Up Your Mind), swears he'll get clean, and she leaves with the only honest answer she has (I Need a Little Longer). Blackout.

Act Two

As the houselights go down, we hear a ruckus from the third row. Robbie and Melissa, both loaded, trading fire as they make their way to the stage: They are still arguing on stage when CLIVE steps out of the dark and stops it cold: they love each other, they just can't get along — and he answers with a song and a chant, cosmic consolation played completely sincere (It'll All Be Better).

Clive provides some wise counsel and Melissa asks where he was twenty years ago; all she got was one piece of advice from her mother — and the duet delivers it (If You Marry a Musician).

Melissa, still pretty high, insists she's fine; Robbie recognizes that she’s still strung out on a muscle relaxant called Manthrax. She knows what she's playing with; she just can't resist the only dance where the floor does half the work (Do the Manthrax) — a fever of a dance number that ends with one light on her, swaying.

As she collapses at the end of a raucous dance sequence, Robbie catches her arm — two people who never have it, holding each other up, two drunks who at least picked the same closing time (You Know What I Can Do). What's left when the noise stops is the truth: they can't keep doing this. She can't say the rest in words, so she picks up a classical guitar and plays it (I Want to See You Again) — and Robbie steps into the warmth and stays.

Robbie pledges his love to Melissa and wants to go home but fears he will be ridiculed as the guy who didn't make it (You Can Worry). Who cares what they say back home: he comes from country music, and he's going back to it — back to making music the old-fashioned way. He apologizes to Clive, and the old man asks to hear one — no Magic Pick, just his own guitar, his own fingers. Robbie hands the pick back and plays a country song clean, unprocessed (What Will You Be Like (When You're Old)) — no light show, no borrowed hands — and it is, quietly, the best he has sounded all show.

Clive pockets the pick — this old troublemaker goes back into retirement. Robbie's going to look up old Sam Hopper, who never made the big time and couldn't care less, and showed folks a real down-home good time — that's the whole gig, always was. The club transforms into a barn dance (They May Not Call It Gospel Music (But Lord It's All for You)), and the principals and the Company dance their way to the edge of the stage and take their bows.
